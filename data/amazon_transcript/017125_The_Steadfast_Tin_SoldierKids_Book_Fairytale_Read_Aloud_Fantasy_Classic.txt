Today we'll be reading The Steadfast Tin Soldier,&nbsp;&nbsp; by Hans Christian Andersen,&nbsp; illustrated by P.J. Lynch. Here's our title page: The Steadfast&nbsp; Tin Soldier. Newly translated from the&nbsp;&nbsp; Danish by Naomi Lewis. Illustrated by P.J. Lynch. Hans Christian Andersen was from Denmark,&nbsp;&nbsp; and he wrote this story all the way back&nbsp; in 1838, in his native language, Danish. Naomi Lewis translated the words into&nbsp; English so we could read the story, too. There were once twenty-five tin&nbsp; soldiers, all of them brothers,&nbsp;&nbsp; for they had been made from the same&nbsp; tin spoon. They shouldered muskets,&nbsp;&nbsp; stared straight ahead, and looked very&nbsp; handsome in their red and blue uniforms. "Tin soldiers!" were the very first words&nbsp; they heard in this world when the lid of&nbsp;&nbsp; their box was taken off. A little boy had&nbsp; shouted those words and clapped his hands&nbsp;&nbsp; happily—he had been given the soldiers as&nbsp; a birthday present. He immediately set them&nbsp;&nbsp; up all in a row on the table. Each soldier&nbsp; was exactly like the next—except for one&nbsp;&nbsp; that only had a single leg. He was the last to be&nbsp; molded, and there wasn't enough tin left. Yet he&nbsp;&nbsp; stood just as well on his one leg as the others&nbsp; did on their two, and he is this story's hero. On the table where they stood were many other&nbsp; toys, but the one everyone noticed first was&nbsp;&nbsp; a magnificent paper castle. Through its little&nbsp; windows you could see right into the rooms. In&nbsp;&nbsp; front of it tiny trees were arranged around a&nbsp; piece of mirrored glass that was meant to look&nbsp;&nbsp; like a lake. Swans made of wax seemed to float on&nbsp; its surface, gazing at their white reflections. The whole scene was enchanting, but the prettiest&nbsp; thing of all was a girl who stood in the open&nbsp;&nbsp; doorway. She, too, was cut out of paper, but&nbsp; her gauzy skirt was of the finest muslin;&nbsp;&nbsp; a narrow blue ribbon crossed over her shoulder&nbsp; like a scarf and was held by a shining sequin&nbsp;&nbsp; almost as large as her face. This charming&nbsp; little lady held her arms stretched out,&nbsp;&nbsp; for she was a dancer--indeed, one of her&nbsp; legs was raised so high in the air that&nbsp;&nbsp; the tin soldier could not see it at all, and he&nbsp; thought that she had only one leg like himself. She would be the perfect wife for me, he thought.&nbsp; But she is so grand! She lives in a castle,&nbsp;&nbsp; and I have only a box--and there are&nbsp; five-and-twenty of us in that! There&nbsp;&nbsp; certainly isn't room for her. Still, I can try&nbsp; to make her acquaintance. So he lay down behind&nbsp;&nbsp; a snuffbox that was on the table. From there&nbsp; he could easily watch the little paper dancer,&nbsp;&nbsp; who continued to stand on one&nbsp; leg without losing her balance. When evening came, all the other tin soldiers&nbsp; were put in their box, and the children went to&nbsp;&nbsp; bed. Then the toys began to play games of their&nbsp; own—they visited each other, and pretended to&nbsp;&nbsp; go to school, and staged battles, and went to&nbsp; parties. The tin soldiers rattled in their box,&nbsp;&nbsp; for they wanted to join in, but they couldn't get&nbsp; the lid off. The nutcrackers turned somersaults,&nbsp;&nbsp; and the chalk squeaked on the&nbsp; chalkboard; there was so much&nbsp;&nbsp; noise that the canary woke up and took part&nbsp; in the playing and chattering--what's more,&nbsp;&nbsp; he spoke in verse. The only two who&nbsp; didn't move were the tin soldier and&nbsp;&nbsp; the little dancer. She continued to stand on&nbsp; the point of her toe, with her arms held out;&nbsp;&nbsp; he stood just as steadily on his single leg--and&nbsp; never once did he take his eyes off her. Then the clock struck twelve. Crack!--the&nbsp; snuffbox lid flew open and up hopped a&nbsp;&nbsp; little black goblin. There was no snuff in the&nbsp; box--it was a kind of trick, a jack-in-the-box. "Tin soldier!" screeched the goblin.&nbsp; "Keep your eyes to yourself!" But the tin soldier pretended not to hear. "All right, just you wait till&nbsp; tomorrow!" said the goblin. When morning came and the children were up again,&nbsp; the tin soldier was placed on the windowsill. The&nbsp;&nbsp; goblin may have been responsible, or perhaps a&nbsp; breeze blowing through the room--in any case,&nbsp;&nbsp; the window suddenly swung open,&nbsp; and out fell the tin soldier,&nbsp;&nbsp; three stories to the ground. It was a&nbsp; frightful fall! His leg pointed upward,&nbsp;&nbsp; his head down, and he landed with his bayonet&nbsp; stuck between two stones in the pavement. The servant girl and the little boy went looking&nbsp; for him in the street, but although they almost&nbsp;&nbsp; stepped on the soldier they somehow failed to see&nbsp; him. If he had called out "Here I am!" they would&nbsp;&nbsp; have found him easily, but he didn't think it&nbsp; proper behavior to shout when he was in uniform. Then it began to rain. The drops fell&nbsp; fast and soon turned into a drenching&nbsp;&nbsp; shower. When it was over, two boys came along. "Look!" said one. "There's a tin&nbsp; soldier. Let's send him out to sea." So they made a boat out of newspaper&nbsp; and put the tin soldier in the middle,&nbsp;&nbsp; and set it in the fast-flowing water in the&nbsp; gutter at the edge of the street. Away he sped,&nbsp;&nbsp; and the two boys ran beside him clapping their&nbsp; hands. What waves there were in that gutter&nbsp;&nbsp; stream! What rolling tides! It had really poured&nbsp; down. The paper boat tossed up and down, sometimes&nbsp;&nbsp; spinning around and around until the soldier felt&nbsp; quite dizzy. But he remained as steadfast as ever,&nbsp;&nbsp; not moving a muscle, still looking straight&nbsp; ahead, still shouldering his musket. All at once the boat entered a tunnel&nbsp; under the sidewalk. Oh, it was dark,&nbsp;&nbsp; as dark as it was in the box at home. Wherever&nbsp; am I going now? the tin soldier wondered. Yes,&nbsp;&nbsp; this must be the goblin's doing. Ah! If only&nbsp; that young lady were here with me in the boat,&nbsp;&nbsp; I wouldn't care if it were twice as dark. Suddenly, from its home in the tunnel, a large water rat rushed out. "Have you got a passport?" it demanded.&nbsp; "No entry without a passport!" But the tin soldier didn't say a word;&nbsp; he only gripped his musket more tightly&nbsp;&nbsp; than ever. The boat rushed onward, with the&nbsp; rat rushing after it, grinding its teeth. It yelled to the sticks and straw,&nbsp;&nbsp; "Stop him! Stop him! He hasn't paid his&nbsp; toll! He hasn't shown his passport!" There was no stopping the boat, though, for the&nbsp; stream ran stronger and stronger. The tin soldier&nbsp;&nbsp; could just see a bright glimpse of daylight&nbsp; far ahead where the end of the tunnel must be,&nbsp;&nbsp; but at the same time he heard a roaring&nbsp; noise which might well have frightened&nbsp;&nbsp; a braver man. Just imagine! At the end of the&nbsp; tunnel the stream thundered down into a great&nbsp;&nbsp; canal. It was as dreadful for him as a plunge&nbsp; down a giant waterfall would be for one of us. But how could he stop? Already he was&nbsp; close to the edge. The boat raced on,&nbsp;&nbsp; and the poor tin soldier held himself as stiffly&nbsp; as he could--no one could say of him that he even&nbsp;&nbsp; blinked an eye. Suddenly the little vessel whirled&nbsp; around three or four times and filled with water&nbsp;&nbsp; right to the brim--what could it do but sink!&nbsp; The tin soldier stood in water up to his neck. Deeper and deeper sank the boat,&nbsp; softer and softer grew the paper,&nbsp;&nbsp; until at last the water closed over the&nbsp; soldier's head. He thought of the lovely&nbsp;&nbsp; little dancer whom he would never see again,&nbsp; and in his ears rang the words of a song: Onward, onward, warrior brave! Fear not danger, nor the grave. Then the paper boat collapsed entirely. The&nbsp;&nbsp; tin soldier fell out and was&nbsp; promptly swallowed by a fish. Oh, how dark it was in the fish's stomach! It&nbsp; was even worse than the tunnel, and much more&nbsp;&nbsp; cramped. But the tin soldier's courage remained&nbsp; unchanged: there he lay, as steadfast as ever,&nbsp;&nbsp; his musket still at his shoulder. The fish&nbsp; thrashed wildly about, twisted and turned,&nbsp;&nbsp; and then became still. Some time later, something&nbsp; flashed through like a streak of lightning--then&nbsp;&nbsp; all around was cheerful daylight, and&nbsp; a voice cried out, "The tin soldier!" The fish had been caught, taken to market,&nbsp; sold, and carried into the kitchen,&nbsp;&nbsp; where the cook had cut it open with a&nbsp; large knife. She picked up the soldier,&nbsp;&nbsp; holding him around his waist with her finger&nbsp; and thumb, and took him into the living room,&nbsp;&nbsp; so that the whole family could see the remarkable&nbsp; character who had traveled inside of a fish. But the tin soldier was not at all proud. They&nbsp; stood him up on the table, and there--well,&nbsp;&nbsp; the world is full of wonders!--he saw that he&nbsp; was in the very same room where his adventures&nbsp;&nbsp; had started. There were the very same children&nbsp; and the very same toys, and there was the fine&nbsp;&nbsp; paper castle with the graceful little dancer&nbsp; at the door. She was still poised on one leg,&nbsp;&nbsp; with the other raised high in the air. Ah,&nbsp; she was steadfast, too. The tin soldier was&nbsp;&nbsp; deeply moved. He would have liked to weep tin&nbsp; tears, but that would not have been soldierly&nbsp;&nbsp; behavior. He looked at her, and she looked&nbsp; at him, but not a word passed between them. And then a strange thing happened.&nbsp; One of the little boys picked up&nbsp;&nbsp; the tin soldier and threw him into the stove. He&nbsp;&nbsp; had no reason for doing this--it must&nbsp; have been the snuffbox goblin's fault. The tin soldier stood framed in a blaze of&nbsp; light. The heat was intense, but whether the&nbsp;&nbsp; heat came from the fire or his burning love, he&nbsp; could not tell. And his bright colors were now&nbsp;&nbsp; gone--but whether they had been washed away by&nbsp; his journey, or by his grief, none could say.&nbsp;&nbsp; He looked at the pretty little dancer, and she&nbsp; looked at him. He felt that he was melting away,&nbsp;&nbsp; but still he stood steadfast, shouldering&nbsp; his musket. Suddenly the door flew open,&nbsp;&nbsp; a gust of air caught the little paper girl,&nbsp; and she flew like a sylph right into the stove,&nbsp;&nbsp; straight to the waiting tin soldier.&nbsp; There she flashed into flame and vanished. The soldier soon melted down into&nbsp; a lump of tin, and the next day,&nbsp;&nbsp; when the maid raked out the ashes, she&nbsp; found him in the shape of a little tin&nbsp;&nbsp; heart. And the dancer? All they found was&nbsp; her sequin and that was as black as soot. What do we notice about this story? First, I notice that, unlike all&nbsp; the other tin soldiers in the box,&nbsp;&nbsp; our main character has only one leg. However,&nbsp; this doesn't stop him from being the best&nbsp;&nbsp; soldier he can be. And when he sees the dancer,&nbsp; and sees how she is always balanced on one leg,&nbsp;&nbsp; it makes him feel they have something in&nbsp; common--it helps him feel closer to her. I also notice that, no matter what happens to&nbsp; the tin soldier, he doesn't complain, and he&nbsp;&nbsp; keeps going, for as long as he can, right to the&nbsp; end. This quality about him--his steadfastness,&nbsp;&nbsp; that he never gives up, is so essential to&nbsp; his character, it's in the title of the story!&nbsp;&nbsp; Sometimes, the title is translated as The Brave&nbsp; Tin Soldier, but it seems to me that bravery and&nbsp;&nbsp; steadfastness have similar meanings, but they&nbsp; are not exactly the same. One way we see the&nbsp;&nbsp; soldier's steadfastness is through his unchanging&nbsp; love for the paper girl. He isn't exactly brave&nbsp;&nbsp; about expressing that love to her--he never&nbsp; tells her how he feels, though it's clear by&nbsp;&nbsp; the end of the story that she knows. I think&nbsp; she feels the same way for him. So while he&nbsp;&nbsp; may not be brave about expressing his love, he&nbsp; is steadfast. His devotion to her never wavers. In addition, I notice that, after the soldier is&nbsp; threatened by the goblin, when bad things happen&nbsp;&nbsp; to him--when he falls out the window, and when he&nbsp; is thrown into the stove--he thinks the goblin is&nbsp;&nbsp; somehow the cause of his misfortune. And while I&nbsp; can't say these things aren't the goblin's fault,&nbsp;&nbsp; I can say that when bad things happen to me, I&nbsp; want an explanation, I want a good reason for it,&nbsp;&nbsp; I want someone to blame... and sometimes,&nbsp; there just isn't anyone. Sometimes,&nbsp;&nbsp; it's a coincidence. Bad luck. Just the way the&nbsp; cookie crumbles. And it can be hard to accept&nbsp;&nbsp; that. I feel like the tin soldier has the&nbsp; same problem with this that I sometimes do. And finally, I can't help but notice how sad&nbsp; the ending is. The first time I read this story,&nbsp;&nbsp; I cried and cried. So why would I want to share&nbsp; such a sad story with you? It seems to me like&nbsp;&nbsp; most stories have happy endings, and I do enjoy&nbsp; those. I like it when good things happen to good&nbsp;&nbsp; people. But you know what? I've been a grownup&nbsp; for a while now, and I've noticed that sometimes,&nbsp;&nbsp; sad things happen, and there's just nothing&nbsp; we can do, but accept it. So it seems to me&nbsp;&nbsp; that stories with sad endings can be good,&nbsp; too--because they help us get ready for the&nbsp;&nbsp; sad things that will someday happen in our own&nbsp; lives. And when these sad things do happen,&nbsp;&nbsp; maybe we can be steadfast, too, just like the&nbsp; tin soldier and the paper girl he loved so much.
