Uncle Jed's Barbershop by Marjorie King&nbsp; Mitchell, illustrated by James Ransome. Jedediah Johnson was my granddaddy's&nbsp; brother. Everybody has their favorite&nbsp;&nbsp; relative. Well, Uncle Jedediah was mine. He used to come by our house every&nbsp; Wednesday night with his clippers.&nbsp;&nbsp; He was the only black barber in the county. Daddy&nbsp; said that before Uncle Jed started cutting hair,&nbsp;&nbsp; he and Granddaddy used to have to&nbsp; go thirty miles to get a haircut. After Uncle Jed cut my daddy's hair,&nbsp; he lathered a short brush with soap&nbsp;&nbsp; and spread it over my daddy's face and shaved&nbsp; him. Then he started over on my granddaddy. I always asked Uncle Jed to cut my&nbsp; hair, but Mama wouldn't let him. So&nbsp;&nbsp; he would run the clippers on the back of&nbsp; my neck and just pretend to cut my hair.&nbsp;&nbsp; He even spread lotion on my neck.&nbsp; I would smell wonderful all day. When he was done, he would pick me up and sit&nbsp; me in his lap and tell me about the barbershop&nbsp;&nbsp; he was going to open one day and about all&nbsp; the fancy equipment that would be in it. The&nbsp;&nbsp; sinks would be so shiny they sparkled, the&nbsp; floor is so clean you could see yourself.&nbsp;&nbsp; He was going to have four barber chairs.&nbsp; And outside was going to be a big, tall,&nbsp;&nbsp; red-and-white barber pole. He&nbsp; told me he was saving up for it. He had been saying the same things&nbsp; for years. Nobody believed him.&nbsp;&nbsp; People didn't have dreams like that in those days. We lived in the South. Most people were&nbsp; poor. My daddy owned a few acres of land&nbsp;&nbsp; and so did a few others. But most people&nbsp; were sharecroppers. That meant they lived&nbsp;&nbsp; in a shack and worked somebody else's&nbsp; land in exchange for a share of the crop. When I was five years old, I got&nbsp; sick. This particular morning,&nbsp;&nbsp; I didn't come into the kitchen while&nbsp; Mama was fixing breakfast. Mama and&nbsp;&nbsp; Daddy couldn't wake me up. My nightgown and the&nbsp; bedclothes were all wet where I had sweated. Mama wrapped me in a blanket while Daddy went&nbsp; outside and hitched the horse to the wagon.&nbsp;&nbsp; We had to travel about twenty miles into town&nbsp; to the hospital. It was midday when we got&nbsp;&nbsp; there. We had to go to the colored waiting&nbsp; room. In those days, they kept blacks and&nbsp;&nbsp; whites separate. There were separate public rest&nbsp; rooms, separate water fountains, separate schools.&nbsp;&nbsp; It was called segregation. So in the hospital,&nbsp; we had to go to the colored waiting room. Even though I was unconscious,&nbsp; the doctors wouldn't look at me&nbsp;&nbsp; until they had finished with all the white&nbsp; patients. When the doctors did examine me,&nbsp;&nbsp; they told my daddy that I needed an operation&nbsp; and that it would cost three hundred dollars. Three hundred dollars was a lot of money&nbsp; in those days. My daddy didn't have that&nbsp;&nbsp; kind of money. And the doctors wouldn't&nbsp; do the operation until they had the money. My mama bundled me back up in the blanket and they&nbsp; took me home. Mama held me in her arms all night.&nbsp;&nbsp; She kept me alive until Daddy found&nbsp; Uncle Jed. He found him early the&nbsp;&nbsp; next morning in the next county on his way to&nbsp; cut somebody's hair. Daddy told him about me. Uncle Jed leaned on his bent&nbsp; cane and stared straight ahead.&nbsp;&nbsp; He told Daddy that the money didn't matter. He&nbsp; couldn't let anything happen to his Sarah Jean. Well, I had the operation. For a long time after&nbsp; that, Uncle Jed came by the house every day to&nbsp;&nbsp; see how I was doing. I know that three hundred&nbsp; dollars delayed him from opening the barbershop. Uncle Jed came awfully close to opening his shop a&nbsp; few years after my operation. He had saved enough&nbsp;&nbsp; money to buy the land and build the building.&nbsp; But he still needed money for the equipment. Anyway, Uncle Jed had come by the house. We&nbsp; had just finished supper when there was a&nbsp;&nbsp; knock on the door. It was Mr. Ernest&nbsp; Walters, a friend of Uncle Jed's. He&nbsp;&nbsp; had come by to tell Uncle Jed about the bank&nbsp; failing. That was where Mr. Walters and Uncle&nbsp;&nbsp; Jed had their money. Uncle Jed had over three&nbsp; thousand dollars in the bank, and it was gone. Uncle Jed just stood there a long time before&nbsp; he said anything. Then he told Mr. Walters&nbsp;&nbsp; that even though he was disappointed, he&nbsp; would just have to start all over again. Talk about some hard times. That was the beginning&nbsp; of the Great Depression. Nobody had much money. But Uncle Jed kept going around to&nbsp; his customers cutting their hair,&nbsp;&nbsp; even though they couldn't pay him. His customers&nbsp; shared with him whatever they had—a hot meal,&nbsp;&nbsp; fresh eggs, vegetables from the garden. And&nbsp; when they were able to pay again, they did. And Uncle Jed started saving all over again. Ol' Uncle Jed finally got his barbershop. He&nbsp; opened it on his seventy-ninth birthday. It&nbsp;&nbsp; had everything, just like he said&nbsp; it would—big comfortable chairs,&nbsp;&nbsp; four cutting stations. You name it! The&nbsp; floors were so clean, they sparkled. On opening day, people came from all over the&nbsp; county. They were Ol' Uncle Jed's customers.&nbsp;&nbsp; He had walked to see them for so many&nbsp; years. That day they all came to him. I believe he cut hair all night and all&nbsp; the next day and the next night and the&nbsp;&nbsp; day after that! That man was so glad to&nbsp; have that shop, he didn't need any sleep. Of course, I was there, too. I wouldn't&nbsp; have missed it for the world. When I&nbsp;&nbsp; sat in one of the big barber chairs, Uncle&nbsp; Jed patted the back of my neck with lotion&nbsp;&nbsp; like he always did. Then he twirled me&nbsp; round and round in the barber chair. Uncle Jed died not long after that, and&nbsp; I think he died a happy man. You see,&nbsp;&nbsp; he made his dream come true even&nbsp; when nobody else believed in it. He taught me to dream, too. Thank you so much for joining me for&nbsp; Read Aloud with Mr. Paul.
