Today we'll be reading Petunia by Roger Duvoisin. Petunia by Roger Duvoisin In the meadow, early one morning, Petunia, the&nbsp; silly goose, went strolling. She ate a bug here,&nbsp;&nbsp; clipped off a clover leaf there, and she&nbsp; picked at the dewdrops on the goldenrod leaves. Then, suddenly, she saw something she had&nbsp; never seen before in the meadow. What was it? Petunia stole closer and closer&nbsp; and sniffed at it from all sides. "By Goosey Gander," she said, "it&nbsp; does not smell like food for a goose.&nbsp;&nbsp; But I believe I have seen such a thing before... "Yes, I have seen one under&nbsp; Bill's arm when he came out&nbsp;&nbsp; of school. It's a Book. That's it. A BOOK! "Come to think of it, just the other day I&nbsp; heard Mr. Pumpkin telling Bill that Books&nbsp;&nbsp; are very precious. 'He who owns Books and&nbsp; loves them is wise.' That is what he said. "He who owns Books and loves them is wise,"&nbsp;&nbsp; repeated Petunia to herself. And she&nbsp; thought as hard and as long as she could. "Well, then," she said at last, "if I&nbsp; take this Book with me, and love it,&nbsp;&nbsp; I will be wise too. And no one will&nbsp; call me a silly goose ever again." So Petunia picked up the Book,&nbsp; and off she went with it. She slept with it... she swam with it. And, knowing that she was so&nbsp; wise, Petunia also became proud,&nbsp;&nbsp; and prouder and prouder... so proud that&nbsp; her neck stretched out several notches. It was King, the rooster, who first&nbsp; noticed the change in Petunia. He said, "Maybe Petunia is not so silly after all.&nbsp;&nbsp; She has a Book. And she looks&nbsp; so wise that she must be so." And the other animals began to believe&nbsp; in Petunia's wisdom too. They asked her&nbsp;&nbsp; for advice and opinions, and Petunia was&nbsp; glad to help—even when she was not asked. Petunia grew still prouder and her&nbsp; neck stretched out another notch. One day petunia heard Clover, the&nbsp; cow, say to King, the rooster,&nbsp;&nbsp; "I wonder what makes your comb&nbsp; so red, King—as red as the barn." "It's my blood," said King.&nbsp; "It's the color of my blood." "Nonsense," said Clover. "I have&nbsp; blood too. But I'm not a red cow.&nbsp;&nbsp; Your comb has been dipped in red&nbsp; barn-paint, that's what makes it so red." "You are both silly, of course," said Petunia.&nbsp; "King, your comb was stuck on by the farmer so&nbsp;&nbsp; he can tell you from the hens and know who lays&nbsp; eggs and who doesn't. Plastic comb, I'd say." And so King never again shook his proud&nbsp; comb in song for fear it might fall off. Poor sad rooster. But Petunia had other things to do. At the chicken coop, Ida, the hen, was&nbsp; cackling excitedly among her chicks. "Oh, Petunia," she said, "my chicks and&nbsp; I have been for a walk in the woods and I&nbsp;&nbsp; think I've lost some of them. The farmer says I&nbsp; had nine but I can't count so very well. Please,&nbsp;&nbsp; wise Petunia, count my chicks&nbsp; to see if they're all here." "Glad to help," said Petunia. "Hm. Let's&nbsp; see. Three chicks at the fountain. Three&nbsp;&nbsp; at the feeder. Three about your legs.&nbsp; Now—three times three? That makes six..." "Six?" asked Ida. "Six! Is that less than nine?" "That's more than nine, not less,"&nbsp; said Petunia. "Lots more, my dear!" "More than nine? Good gracious! As if I&nbsp; hadn't enough worries with my own nine&nbsp;&nbsp; chicks. And where do those other chicks come&nbsp; from? Oh, dear, I'll never be happy again." Poor worried Ida. But Petunia had other things to do. In the meadow she discovered part of Noisy,&nbsp; the dog, sticking out of a hole in the ground. "Help! Help!" cried Noisy. "I&nbsp; stuck my head in this rabbit hole,&nbsp;&nbsp; and now it won't come out. Help!" "Glad to help," said Petunia. "It's a good thing&nbsp; I know what hunters do to get stubborn animals&nbsp;&nbsp; out of holes in the ground. They smoke them out.&nbsp; Wait until I fetch some sticks and some matches. And so, wise Petunia built a fire in the other&nbsp; end of the hole and fanned it well with the Book. Her trick worked nicely.&nbsp; Noisy, choking with smoke,&nbsp;&nbsp; jerked his head out of the hole&nbsp; and ran off howling with pain. His nose was singed with the fire,&nbsp; and his ears were cut and bruised. Poor moaning dog. But Petunia had other things to do. Beside the hedgerow she met Straw, the&nbsp; horse, who was in pain from a toothache. "Petunia," groaned Straw, "I'm dying. Surely, with&nbsp; your wisdom, you can stop this horrible pain." "Glad to help," said Petunia. "Open your mouth.&nbsp;&nbsp; Why... you poor Straw... all these&nbsp; teeth! No wonder you have a toothache. "Look at me. Do I have teeth? Of course not. So&nbsp; I have no toothache. I am going to stop that pain&nbsp;&nbsp; right now. I am going to pull all those teeth&nbsp; out. All of them. Let me get some pliers..." But Straw would not wait for the pliers.&nbsp; He was so afraid to lose his teeth that&nbsp;&nbsp; he never talked of his toothache to&nbsp; another soul. He suffered in silence. Poor forlorn horse. But Petunia had other things to do. Cotton, the kitten, went up the&nbsp; tree but could not come down.&nbsp;&nbsp; While he miaowed and miaowed,&nbsp; his friends called for Petunia. "Glad to help," said Petunia.&nbsp; "I know just what to do.&nbsp;&nbsp; Since none of you is tall enough to reach&nbsp; Cotton, all of you will do it together.&nbsp;&nbsp; Donkey on top of Clover, Pig on top&nbsp; of Donkey, and so on up. Simple." So Donkey climbed on top of Clover; Pig on top of&nbsp; Donkey; Goat on top of Pig; Sheep on top of Goat;&nbsp;&nbsp; Piggy on top of Sheep; Turkey on top of Piggy;&nbsp; Duck on top of Turkey; Hen on top of Duck... Suddenly Clover cried out,&nbsp; "Stop! My legs feel wobbly." And she sat... ...and Donkey and the rest fell into a heap,&nbsp;&nbsp; and Cotton was so scared that he fell on&nbsp; top of them. They were all full of bumps. "Well," said Petunia, "Cotton is down." So he was, poor bruised kitten. But Petunia had other things to&nbsp; do. Getting prouder all the time,&nbsp;&nbsp; she felt her neck stretch further out. She now wandered down the meadow, where she&nbsp; found some other friends gathered around a box. "Ah, wise Petunia!" they shouted. "We&nbsp; found this box in the ditch beside the&nbsp;&nbsp; road. Maybe it's food, Petunia. Please&nbsp; tell us what the writing on it says." "Glad to help," said Petunia.&nbsp; "Now, let's see... Why, CANDIES.&nbsp;&nbsp; That's what it says on that box. Yes,&nbsp; candies. You may eat them. Yes, of course." No sooner had Petunia given the word&nbsp; than seven greedy mouths tore up the&nbsp;&nbsp; box and grabbed the candies out of it, and... BOOM What a sight the animals were! Some were burned. Some were bruised. Straw still suffered in&nbsp; silence. Noisy still moaned.&nbsp;&nbsp; Ida still worried about her chicks.&nbsp; King still brooded over his comb. All the barnyard was in trouble, and all because of Petunia. Petunia's pride and wisdom had&nbsp; exploded with the firecrackers. Her neck had shrunk back to its&nbsp; old size and was all bandaged up.&nbsp;&nbsp; She was the most downhearted of all, for&nbsp; she saw now that she was not a bit wise. But suddenly Petunia spied the Book. The&nbsp; firecrackers had blown it open so that the&nbsp;&nbsp; pages showed. She had never seen them before.&nbsp; Now she saw that there was something written&nbsp;&nbsp; inside the Book which she could not read. So she&nbsp; sat down and thought and thought and thought,&nbsp;&nbsp; until at last she sighed, "Now I understand. It&nbsp; was not enough to carry wisdom under my wing.&nbsp;&nbsp; I must put it in my mind and in my heart.&nbsp; And to do that I must learn to read." Petunia was filled with joy.
