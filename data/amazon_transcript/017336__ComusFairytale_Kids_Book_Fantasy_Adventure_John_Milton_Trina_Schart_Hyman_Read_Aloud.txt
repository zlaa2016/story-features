Comus, adapted by Margaret Hodges, from&nbsp; A Masque at Ludlow Castle by John Milton,&nbsp;&nbsp; illustrated by Trina Schart Hyman Once upon a time there were three children&nbsp; who lived with their father and mother in a&nbsp;&nbsp; castle at Ludlow, near the river Severn. Alice&nbsp; was the oldest. Then came John, then Thomas.&nbsp;&nbsp; In a wild woods not far from the river,&nbsp;&nbsp; the children often played hide-and-seek or&nbsp; roamed about looking for flowers and berries. But one day, as they went along and went along in&nbsp; the wild woods, they strayed too far from home.&nbsp;&nbsp; Night was falling, and the forest&nbsp; was closing in around them.&nbsp;&nbsp; Soon the path disappeared among dark bushes. Alice sat down to rest under a tree. "I am too tired to go farther,"&nbsp; she said to her brothers.&nbsp;&nbsp; "Perhaps you can find the path. I&nbsp; will wait here until you come back." "We'll bring you some berries and&nbsp; some water to drink," said John,&nbsp;&nbsp; and off he went with Thomas. Soon&nbsp; their voices and footsteps died away. All alone Alice waited and waited,&nbsp; but her brothers did not come back.&nbsp;&nbsp; Then in the distance she heard the&nbsp; shouts of a wild, savage crowd. The noise seemed to be coming closer. Alice&nbsp; trembled, but she was too brave to cry.&nbsp;&nbsp; Suddenly there was silence. Looking out&nbsp; into the darkness of the deep woods,&nbsp;&nbsp; Alice saw evil faces peering out, watching&nbsp; her, but she did not dare to move. "Perhaps John and Thomas are not far off,"&nbsp; she thought. "I'll sing to keep up my courage,&nbsp;&nbsp; and maybe my brothers will hear me." But while Alice sang, someone else&nbsp; was listening and saying to himself,&nbsp;&nbsp; Ah! My nightingale! I will&nbsp; catch you and keep you!" This was Comus, a wicked magician. The sounds&nbsp; Alice had heard were the voices of monsters,&nbsp;&nbsp; once men and women, whom&nbsp; Comus had turned into beasts.&nbsp;&nbsp; He had learned his spells from his mother, Circe,&nbsp; a witch, and his father, Bacchus, the god of wine. Comus stepped toward Alice, throwing magic dust&nbsp; into the air so that her eyes were dazzled. To her he looked like a harmless villager,&nbsp; wearing a homespun cloak and carrying a staff.&nbsp;&nbsp; In the dark of night she did not&nbsp; see the two little horns among&nbsp;&nbsp; his thick curly locks or the cloven&nbsp; hooves below his shepherd's dress. He greeted her courteously. "Lady, how came you to be in&nbsp; the wild woods all alone?" "My brothers left me to rest&nbsp; here while they went to find&nbsp;&nbsp; water and some berries to refresh me," said Alice. "Are they grown men or boys?" asked Comus. "They are only lads," she told him. "I saw two lads about nightfall,&nbsp; due west of here," said Comus. "They&nbsp;&nbsp; were a handsome pair and were picking&nbsp; berries. Let me help you find them." "You must know the paths well to find the&nbsp; way on such a dark night," said Alice. "I know all the paths," the stranger&nbsp; answered. "I will find your brothers,&nbsp;&nbsp; Lady, or if not, I can lead you to a humble&nbsp; but honest cottage where you will be safe." "I trust your word," said Alice. "I&nbsp; know that simple folk are often more&nbsp;&nbsp; honest and courteous than great&nbsp; people at the king's court." Soon after Comus had led Alice&nbsp; away, deeper into the woods,&nbsp;&nbsp; her two brothers returned&nbsp; to where they had left her. "Where can she be?" Thomas said anxiously.&nbsp; "Is she wandering about in the dark and&nbsp;&nbsp; damp among sharp burrs and thistles?&nbsp; What if someone has stolen her away?" "How will we ever find our way home? If only&nbsp; the moon or stars would shine!" John sighed.&nbsp;&nbsp; "If we could even see the flicker of&nbsp; one small candle among the trees!" "If we could hear sheep bleating or&nbsp; a cock crowing, we would know we were&nbsp;&nbsp; near a shepherd's cottage," said Thomas.&nbsp; "But it is worse for Alice, all alone." John answered, "Alice is not the&nbsp; sort to be afraid for no reason." "True enough," said Thomas. "Only don't forget&nbsp; the old story about the dragon who guards the&nbsp;&nbsp; golden apple tree to keep it safe from robbers.&nbsp; Alice is beautiful, and beauty should be guarded." "Still, let's hope rather than fear," said&nbsp; John. "I have heard that no evil spirit,&nbsp;&nbsp; no witch or ghost or goblin or wicked fairy,&nbsp; can harm a truly good person, as Alice is. Listen! I hear shouting far off." "I hear it, too," said Thomas. "Maybe it is someone lost, like us,&nbsp;&nbsp; or maybe a forester," John answered. "Unless&nbsp; it is a robber calling to other robbers." "Heaven help my sister!" said Thomas. "There is the noise again, coming nearer.&nbsp; Let's draw our swords and stand on guard." While the two brothers stood with&nbsp; swords drawn, someone came toward them.&nbsp;&nbsp; He looked like a shepherd, and Thomas&nbsp; thought that he had seen his face before. "What are you doing here, good shepherd?"&nbsp; he asked. "Have you come from Ludlow Castle,&nbsp;&nbsp; looking for a lost sheep, and found us instead?" But this was not a shepherd. It was a Good Spirit&nbsp; who had come from the other end of the rainbow,&nbsp;&nbsp; sent from heaven to guard those in danger.&nbsp;&nbsp; He had taken off his rainbow robes&nbsp; and put on a shepherd's cloak. "I did not come to find a lost sheep but to find&nbsp; you and your sister," he said. "Where is she?" "We lost her, shepherd," said&nbsp; John. "It was not our fault." "Then I'm even more fearful than before I met&nbsp; you," said the Spirit. "In the very center of&nbsp;&nbsp; these wild woods lives a powerful magician. Comus&nbsp; is his name. He is as skillful in witchcraft as&nbsp;&nbsp; his mother Circe, and, like his father Bacchus, he&nbsp; offers every thirsty wanderer a drink that changes&nbsp;&nbsp; a man or a woman into a monster with the face&nbsp; of a beast. When I tend my flocks on the hills&nbsp;&nbsp; at night, I can hear Comus's rabble, howling like&nbsp; wolves or tigers. Tonight I heard them again. Then&nbsp;&nbsp; suddenly they were silent and I heard someone&nbsp; singing as sweetly as a nightingale. I knew it&nbsp;&nbsp; was the voice of the Lady, your dear sister. Down&nbsp; the hill I ran until I found the place where that&nbsp;&nbsp; deceitful wizard must have met the innocent Lady.&nbsp; Of course she thought he was some good villager&nbsp;&nbsp; who would lead her to her brothers. I came swift&nbsp; as the sparkle of a star, but not swift enough." "She's in danger!" cried Thomas. "John,&nbsp; you said no harm could come to her!" "And I still say so!" John answered. "But&nbsp; let's go to her. I'll draw my sword against&nbsp;&nbsp; that cursed magician, even if he has all&nbsp; the grisly legions of hell around him!" "I love your courage," said the Spirit.&nbsp;&nbsp; "But one wave of Comus's magic staff could take&nbsp; all your courage away. I must go with you." "How can you help us?" asked John. "I am carrying a little root called Haemony," said&nbsp; the Good Spirit. "It grows in our country fields,&nbsp;&nbsp; but though it is not beautiful and is dark and&nbsp; prickly, it has wonderful powers to protect&nbsp;&nbsp; anyone against enchantments. I will use it&nbsp; to open the door of Comus's hidden palace,&nbsp;&nbsp; and I will give each of you a piece&nbsp; of Haemony to carry with you." "If you will open the door, we are not afraid&nbsp; to attack Comus by ourselves," said John. "Then you must rush boldly in and break&nbsp; his crystal glass," the Spirit told him.&nbsp;&nbsp; "Attack Comus with your swords and seize his&nbsp; staff, for all his power is in the staff.&nbsp;&nbsp; You will not strike in vain, and&nbsp; once you have defeated Comus,&nbsp;&nbsp; his monsters will disappear like smoke. I will&nbsp; be nearby if you need me. You will see me again." The Good Spirit showed the brothers a path&nbsp; through the woods to a round green hill.&nbsp;&nbsp; He touched it with the root of Haemony&nbsp; and a door opened into a dark passage. The Good Spirit disappeared, but John and Thomas&nbsp; went forward boldly. The door closed behind them. There were neither windows nor candles&nbsp; in the passage, but in a strange kind&nbsp;&nbsp; of twilight the brothers could see that the&nbsp; walls and roof were thickly set with jewels.&nbsp;&nbsp; The air was warm. At the end of the passage the&nbsp; brothers stopped, half hidden in the shadows.&nbsp;&nbsp; Before them was a fantastic sight—a&nbsp; spacious hall, its roof supported by&nbsp;&nbsp; pillars of gold and silver wreathed with&nbsp; diamonds and emeralds, rubies and pearls.&nbsp;&nbsp; In the middle of the roof, hung from a golden&nbsp; chain, was a great lamp made of one enormous&nbsp;&nbsp; pearl in which a red jewel spun round and round,&nbsp; lighting the hall like a setting sun. Comus's&nbsp;&nbsp; monsters were crowded around tables of rich and&nbsp; delicious food. They were dressed in fine clothes&nbsp;&nbsp; with jeweled belts and sparkling embroideries, but&nbsp; above all the splendor, the faces of beasts—apes&nbsp;&nbsp; and wolves, and hogs, and some beasts never&nbsp; known on land or sea—chattered and howled and grunted. Then the two brothers saw Alice. In the midst&nbsp; of this fearful place, she sat on a high,&nbsp;&nbsp; carved chair, and Comus stood before&nbsp; her, holding out his crystal glass. He&nbsp;&nbsp; was no longer disguised as a kindly shepherd, but&nbsp; showed himself as he really was, a crafty demon. "See, Lady, you cannot move from my enchanted&nbsp; chair," he said. "I have caught you,&nbsp;&nbsp; my nightingale, and if I wave my staff, you will&nbsp; never move again. But if you drink from my glass,&nbsp;&nbsp; all my riches are yours. You will be queen in&nbsp; this palace, and happier than you have ever been." "You lie," said Alice bravely. "Was this the safe&nbsp; cottage you told me of? Who are these monsters?&nbsp;&nbsp; You want to trap me as you have trapped&nbsp; them. I will not touch your food and drink.&nbsp;&nbsp; If I do, you will keep me&nbsp; here forever. Heaven help me!" Again Comus held out his glass, smiling. "Come," he said, "no more of&nbsp; this nonsense. Take one sip!" At this, the brothers rushed in with swords drawn.&nbsp;&nbsp; They seized Comus's glass and hurled it to&nbsp; the floor, breaking it into a thousand pieces. "We know who you are!" they cried.&nbsp; "Come and face us, foul fiend!" But Comus, waving his staff, fled with his&nbsp; monsters into a dark tunnel and was gone. Just then the Good Spirit appeared. "The false enchanter was too quick for you,"&nbsp; he said to the brothers. "He has escaped with&nbsp;&nbsp; his staff and taken his wicked power with it.&nbsp; And there sits the Lady, bound by a spell that&nbsp;&nbsp; even Haemony cannot break. But wait! There may&nbsp; be another way. Not far from here, in the river&nbsp;&nbsp; Severn, lives a gentle nymph who is as powerful as&nbsp; Comus. Her name is Sabrina, goddess of the stream.&nbsp;&nbsp; Perhaps she can unlock the charm&nbsp; and free the Lady. I'll call her." And by good magic, as the Spirit called, a sweet voice&nbsp; answered, clear as rippling water,&nbsp;&nbsp; "I am here." It was Sabrina herself, who&nbsp; had come in time of need, kind and smiling,&nbsp;&nbsp; surrounded by bright water nymphs,&nbsp; into the dark magic of Comus's palace.&nbsp;&nbsp; Sabrina sprinkled drops of pure water on&nbsp; Alice's eyes and ears, on her nostrils, lips,&nbsp;&nbsp; and fingertips. Then she laid her cool, moist&nbsp; hands on the enchanted chair, and Alice rose. Free and unharmed, she ran to kiss&nbsp; her brothers, and to thank Sabrina. "Come, Lady," said the Good Spirit, "we&nbsp; must fly from this cursed place. I will&nbsp;&nbsp; lead you safely home to your father's castle,&nbsp; where all are waiting for you anxiously." Back they went together through the&nbsp; dark passage, into the wild woods,&nbsp;&nbsp; by easy paths to Ludlow Castle, into&nbsp; the arms of their father and mother.&nbsp;&nbsp; The whole countryside rejoiced&nbsp; that the children had been found. The Good Spirit, his task done, waved farewell,&nbsp; and the children saw him in his rainbow robes,&nbsp;&nbsp; flying up to his own home in&nbsp; the blue field of the sky. As for Alice and John and Thomas, in later years&nbsp; they sometimes ventured into the wild woods,&nbsp;&nbsp; but they never again saw Comus. Author's Note The oldest of all the old English fairy&nbsp; tales may be the one called "Childe Roland."&nbsp;&nbsp; The poet John Milton liked the story&nbsp; so much that he retold it as a play,&nbsp;&nbsp; which he called "a masque"&nbsp; because it was full of disguises. Three children, Alice and John and&nbsp; Thomas Egerton, acted in the play,&nbsp;&nbsp; which was performed for the first time in their&nbsp; father's castle at Ludlow on Michaelmas Night,&nbsp;&nbsp; 1634. This was a festival day in England. Alice played the part of The Lady.&nbsp; John and Thomas were The Two Brothers.&nbsp;&nbsp; We do not know who acted the part of Sabrina,&nbsp; the nymph of the river Severn that flows near&nbsp;&nbsp; Ludlow Castle. A professional actor may have&nbsp; played the part of Comus, the wicked wizard. The towers of Ludlow Castle still stand,&nbsp;&nbsp; Comus is still performed there to this&nbsp; very day, and the story lives on forever.
