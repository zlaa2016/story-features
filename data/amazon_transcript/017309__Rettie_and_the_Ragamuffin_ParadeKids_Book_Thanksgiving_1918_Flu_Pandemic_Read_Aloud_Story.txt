Rettie and the Ragamuffin&nbsp; Parade, A Thanksgiving Story,&nbsp;&nbsp; written by Trinka Hakes Noble and&nbsp; illustrated by David C. Gardner All the tenement children on New York's Lower East Side couldn't wait for the Ragamuffin Parade on&nbsp; Thanksgiving morning. But no one was more&nbsp;&nbsp; excited than a young girl named Loretta&nbsp; Stanowski, whom everyone called Rettie. All you had to do was dress up like a beggar&nbsp; in old ragged clothes and parade down Broadway. For Rettie, dressing up was easy. She already&nbsp; had holes in her shoes and worn, patched clothes. Then fancy uptown people would line the streets&nbsp; and call, "Here come the little ragamuffins!" If you held out your hand and asked,&nbsp; "Have ya anythin' for Thanksgiving?" they'd give you a penny. But the best part of the Ragamuffin&nbsp; Parade happened on busy street corners. Handfuls of pennies were tossed in the&nbsp; air, then all the ragamuffin children&nbsp;&nbsp; would scramble for them. It was called a&nbsp; penny scramble, and sometimes it got rough. Amelia and little Katalina looked worried. "Rettie, this Thanksgiving, are you going&nbsp; to scramble?" they asked their older sister. This year Rettie was determined to join in. She wanted to buy something special&nbsp; for their Thanksgiving dinner. "Don't worry," Rettie said&nbsp; as she rocked baby Sebastian. "I'm going to scramble hard and&nbsp; bring home lots of pennies for us." But that November, in 1918, a great war raged&nbsp; in Europe. Many American men had gone to fight.&nbsp;&nbsp; In America, another war was sweeping across the&nbsp; land, one with an invisible enemy... influenza. Quarantine signs had been posted, large&nbsp; gatherings banned, and schools closed.&nbsp;&nbsp; Rettie worried. What if they cancelled the&nbsp; Ragamuffin Parade? She needed those pennies! Rettie's mama had been sick for&nbsp; a month, weakened by consumption. So Rettie did all the cleaning,&nbsp; washing, shopping, and cooking. She also washed rags for the&nbsp; ragpicker for two pennies a bundle. After all, she was nine, the&nbsp; oldest. Who else would take&nbsp;&nbsp; care of the family while Papa was&nbsp; a soldier, so far away in Europe? Besides, Mama would get better soon, wouldn't&nbsp; she? But there was no time for worry. Rettie hurried toward the pushcart market&nbsp; on Hester Street to do the shopping. At first things seemed the same. The crowded,&nbsp; noisy hustle and bustle was there as always.&nbsp;&nbsp; Back alley garbage stench still mingled&nbsp; with the smell of horses waiting for their&nbsp;&nbsp; wagons to be unloaded. The squeak, squeak,&nbsp; squeak of the ragpicker's cart sounded the&nbsp;&nbsp; same as he called up to fire escapes and&nbsp; open windows, "Rags? Rags? Got any rags?" And someone was always cooking cabbage. But there were fewer boys pitching bottle caps&nbsp; on stoops and fewer girls playing hopscotch. Due to the influenza pandemic, many children&nbsp; had become orphans overnight and were living&nbsp;&nbsp; in back alleys or taken off to orphanages.&nbsp; Rettie had heard bad things about orphanages.&nbsp;&nbsp; She just couldn't let that happen to&nbsp; her little sisters and baby brother. Over on Broadway, the uptown trolley still rattled&nbsp; and clanged, but fewer people rushed to board,&nbsp;&nbsp; and some of them wore masks. And on Hester&nbsp; Street, there were fewer pushcart merchants. "What, no pickled herring today?" complained&nbsp; Mrs. Goldstein. "How can I feed my family?" "Schmidt's whole family is quarantined,"&nbsp; Mrs. Petrov said with a shrug. "And no beets! I ask you, how can&nbsp; I make my borscht without beets?" But Zimmermann's bread cart was still there.&nbsp; Rettie selected two hard, stale loaves. "That'll be six cents," said Mr. Zimmermann. "But my little sisters will break their teeth&nbsp; on these. I'll give you two cents each." Then she hurried to Clancy's for one&nbsp; small cabbage and three mealy potatoes,&nbsp;&nbsp; which she got for a penny each. Then O'Leary's&nbsp; for two ladles of milk for baby Sebastian. Rettie had spent all her pennies, but&nbsp; she lingered at Rossetti's fruit cart&nbsp;&nbsp; and secretly ran her hand over&nbsp; the smooth, cool orange pumpkins. Then she breathed in the sweet ripe smell&nbsp; of apples, wishing she could buy them. Mrs. Goldstein haggled.&nbsp; "Rossetti, these apples fresh?" "Just shipped down the Hudson&nbsp; River from upstate this morning." Just then Rossetti spied Rettie. "Shoo, girl! Only paying customers!" he&nbsp; exclaimed, flapping his long apron at her. When Rettie got home, a health service nurse was&nbsp;&nbsp; posting a quarantine sign&nbsp; on Mrs. Klumpenthal's door. "But, Nurse Pauline," Mrs. Klumpenthal&nbsp; pleaded. "I manage this building. I must clean the stairs and stoop and empty the&nbsp; ash barrel or the health department will fine me!" Nurse Pauline was tall, wearing a uniform—a&nbsp; starched white collar, apron, and cap. Rettie had never seen anyone so tidy. She&nbsp; carried a black bag and spoke with authority. "Ma'am, we must contain the&nbsp; spread of this deadly influenza. No one can leave your apartment." Quickly Rettie did addition in&nbsp; her head then made an offer. "I'll clean the stairways on all four floors each&nbsp; day, and the stoop for twenty-five cents a week." With Nurse Pauline standing right&nbsp; there, Mrs. Klumpenthal couldn't say no. Nurse Pauline turned to Rettie, felt her forehead, checked for swollen glands, and said,&nbsp; "Stick out your tongue, child. Good.&nbsp;&nbsp; No influenza. Which is your apartment?&nbsp; I'm checking everyone in the building." When Nurse Pauline entered&nbsp; the tiny two-room apartment,&nbsp;&nbsp; she saw neatly folded piles of clean rags and&nbsp; more clean rags drying on the fire escape. "Does your mama wash rags?" she asked. "No, ma'am. I do. Mama's been&nbsp; sick and Papa's away at the war." Nurse Pauline saw how clean everything was, and&nbsp; how well cared for the younger children were. "You've been doing all this, child?&nbsp; If your mother doesn't get better,&nbsp;&nbsp; and with your father away,&nbsp; do you know what that means?" Rettie lowered her head and almost cried. "Yes, ma'am." She sighed. "An orphanage." "Well, let's see what I can do." Nurse&nbsp; Pauline examined Mama. "Good. No influenza." She gave Mama some medicine, then&nbsp; instructed Rettie on the dosage. "Now, let me inspect your hands,"&nbsp; she said briskly. Rettie's hands&nbsp;&nbsp; were spotlessly clean because&nbsp; they were always in soapy water. "Now keep these little ones inside, but let in&nbsp; fresh air and sunshine each day," she ordered. "Keep everyone's hands clean and,&nbsp; most important, let your mother rest." Then Nurse Pauline gently touched&nbsp; Rettie's shoulder and spoke softly. "Child, if you follow these instructions,&nbsp; you'd make a fine nurse someday." So Rettie made a little pretend school under the table. There she taught Amelia and Katalina&nbsp; their numbers and letters while baby&nbsp;&nbsp; Sebastian napped in her lap.&nbsp; She told stories, hummed songs,&nbsp;&nbsp; and played little games, keeping&nbsp; everyone quiet so Mama could rest. At four o'clock each morning&nbsp; while everyone was asleep,&nbsp;&nbsp; Rettie washed the stairs and railings,&nbsp; swept the halls, and scrubbed the stoop. The only thing that kept her going was the hope&nbsp; that the Ragamuffin Parade wouldn't be cancelled. Slowly, with Nurse Pauline's&nbsp; medicine, Mama improved,&nbsp;&nbsp; until one day Rettie helped her walk&nbsp; slowly to the window for fresh air. On that special day, November 11,&nbsp; 1918, as Mama sat by the open window,&nbsp;&nbsp; they heard newsboys all over the Lower East&nbsp; Side shouting, "Extra! Extra! The War is over!" Then church bells started ringing all over New York. And for the first time in weeks&nbsp; Mama smiled as Rettie, Amelia,&nbsp;&nbsp; and Katalina danced around baby Sebastian. Papa would be coming home! Over the next few weeks, a frigid wind blew&nbsp; through New York and slowed the influenza.&nbsp;&nbsp; World War I was over, and with the return of cold&nbsp; weather, the great influenza pandemic diminished. America had much to be thankful for this&nbsp; Thanksgiving. So President Woodrow Wilson declared&nbsp;&nbsp; that the 28th of November would be Thanksgiving&nbsp; Day for all Americans to celebrate as one. But no one was more thankful than Rettie. On&nbsp; Thanksgiving morning she dressed in her everyday&nbsp;&nbsp; patched clothes and worn shoes and proudly marched&nbsp; in the Ragamuffin Parade with her face beaming. Many people gave her pennies. And, on&nbsp; the corner of Broadway and Broome Street,&nbsp;&nbsp; she bravely entered the penny scramble and&nbsp; added a fistful of pennies to her pockets. Then she ran to Hester Street with her pockets&nbsp; jingling. Maybe she could catch Mr. Rossetti. He was just leaving. "Wait, Mr. Rossetti! I need five apples!" "Only small ones left," he&nbsp; said. "Three cents each, okay?" He picked out the best five apples left&nbsp; as Rettie counted out fifteen pennies. "How much for that orange pumpkin?" she asked. "Oh, too much for you," he said,&nbsp; sighing. "A whole ten cents." Rettie proudly counted out ten more&nbsp; pennies. She didn't even bargain with him. Then she gathered the apples and&nbsp; pumpkin in her apron and ran home. On that Thanksgiving Day, in 1918, there were many&nbsp; grand dinners and celebrations across America,&nbsp;&nbsp; but none was better than the one set in a&nbsp; small two-room tenement on the Lower East Side. By each bowl of stewed cabbage sat a shiny&nbsp; red apple. And in the center of the table,&nbsp;&nbsp; like the guest of honor,&nbsp; was a small orange pumpkin. And at that moment, there was no&nbsp; world war, no filth and sickness,&nbsp;&nbsp; no hunger and hardship. Only a young girl's&nbsp; heart filled with the hope of Thanksgiving. Author's Note The Ragamuffin parade was introduced to New&nbsp; York City in 1870, brought by the many European&nbsp;&nbsp; immigrants who had settled there. Because&nbsp; they wanted to fit in with American holidays,&nbsp;&nbsp; they paired the Ragamuffin Parade with&nbsp; Thanksgiving. On Thanksgiving morning,&nbsp;&nbsp; New York City children would dress up&nbsp; like beggars and parade through the&nbsp;&nbsp; streets with their hands out asking,&nbsp; "Have you anything for Thanksgiving?" People called them ragamuffins and would give them&nbsp; a penny or fruit. The immigrant children who lived&nbsp;&nbsp; in the tenements loved the Ragamuffin Parade&nbsp; because they could fill a pocket with pennies. But as the years went by, Halloween&nbsp; became more popular in America. Children dressed up in costumes,&nbsp; marched in Halloween parades,&nbsp;&nbsp; and went trick-or-treating for candy.&nbsp; The Ragamuffin Parade fell out of favor. Many immigrant children grew up to be&nbsp; employed by Macy's department store in&nbsp;&nbsp; Midtown Manhattan. They remembered&nbsp; the Ragamuffin Parade fondly. Some historians believe that these employees&nbsp; asked Mr. Macy if he would sponsor a parade&nbsp;&nbsp; for the children of New York City on&nbsp; Thanksgiving morning. And so, in 1924,&nbsp;&nbsp; the first Macy's Thanksgiving Day Parade&nbsp; took place. It was so successful that it&nbsp;&nbsp; became an annual event, which&nbsp; Americans enjoy to this day. In 1918 the great influenza pandemic&nbsp; swept across America and around the world. This deadly strain of flu killed an&nbsp; estimated fifty million people worldwide. In comparison, sixteen&nbsp; million died in World War I. When I was in grade school, an influenza epidemic&nbsp; called the Asian flu swept across America. Many children became sick, including me.&nbsp; That fall I missed four weeks of school,&nbsp;&nbsp; and I also missed Halloween! So&nbsp; my parents bought me a chocolate&nbsp;&nbsp; candy bar and placed it next to my&nbsp; bed to look at until I got better. I placed this story during America's&nbsp; 1918 great influenza pandemic,&nbsp;&nbsp; on New York's Lower East Side,&nbsp; thinking about how the tenement&nbsp;&nbsp; children would miss the Ragamuffin&nbsp; Parade, just as I missed Halloween.
