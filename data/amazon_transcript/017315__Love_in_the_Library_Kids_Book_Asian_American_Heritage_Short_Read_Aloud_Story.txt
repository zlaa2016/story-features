Today we'll be reading Love in the Library by Maggie Tokuda-Hall, illustrated by Yas Imamura. Love in the Library by Maggie Tokuda-Hall, illustrated by Yas Imamura Tama did not like the desert. She brushed the dust from her&nbsp; eyes as she walked to the library.&nbsp;&nbsp; The barbed wire fences and guard&nbsp; towers cast long shadows over her path. She always did her best not to look at the guards. Tama had taken the job in the&nbsp; library because she liked books.&nbsp;&nbsp; She didn't know how to be a librarian,&nbsp; but that didn't matter. In the camps,&nbsp;&nbsp; people did the jobs that needed&nbsp; doing, and that was that. "Good morning, Tama," a voice boomed. It was George. Like every other&nbsp; day, George was waiting at the&nbsp;&nbsp; door for Tama with the stack of books&nbsp; he'd checked out only the day before. "Good morning, George," Tama replied. He waited for her to unlock the library,&nbsp; and then he followed her inside. Tama and George had been in Minidoka for&nbsp; a year already. She should have graduated&nbsp;&nbsp; college the previous summer, but instead,&nbsp; Tama was here. All the Japanese Americans&nbsp;&nbsp; from the West Coast were in prison camps like&nbsp; Minidoka. Elderly people, children, babies. It didn't matter who you were,&nbsp;&nbsp; just what you were—and being Japanese&nbsp; American then was treated like a crime. And though each camp was&nbsp; different, they were all the same. Uncomfortable and unjust. In the summer, it was brutally hot. In the winter, it was brutally cold. Rain in the fall and spring left mud&nbsp; around the latrines they all needed to use. Whole families shared single rooms. No one had any privacy. Tama did not know when she would&nbsp; leave Minidoka—if she ever would. But there was nothing to be done about that. So she worked in the library and&nbsp; watched as each day passed her by. Some days, many people would come to the&nbsp; library and borrow something to read. Other days, especially when it snowed, hardly&nbsp; anyone would brave the cold just for a book. But most days were the same. Tama kept her eyes down and tried not to&nbsp; think about the life she used to have. There was a word that described&nbsp; this, Tama knew: constant. Constant questions. Constant worries. Constant fear. But then, there was also George. George and his big smile were constant. And the books were constant company.&nbsp; Which was nice. Tama loved books.&nbsp;&nbsp; Caught in their pages were worlds bursting&nbsp; with color and light, love and fairness. Pressed between their covers were words that&nbsp; planted seeds in the garden of Tama's mind.&nbsp;&nbsp; How magical that—even in Minidoka—such&nbsp;&nbsp; a small little library could fit&nbsp; so much inside of its four walls! Tama kept a word for that, too,&nbsp; nestled close to her heart. "Miraculous," she whispered. "What was that?" asked George. His voice was so big it barely&nbsp; fit in the library. Tama blushed. Then she held up her finger&nbsp; to remind him of the rules.&nbsp;&nbsp; They were in a library, after all.&nbsp; George just went on grinning at her. Tama wanted to smile back. She tried to focus on the&nbsp; things that made her smile— the boys outside, hollering and&nbsp; cheering as they played baseball. The books themselves and the smell of their pages. But she could not make herself smile. She sighed. "What's wrong?" George asked. "Nothing. Or everything," Tama said. She did not need to tell him what she&nbsp; meant. He knew. Everyone in Minidoka knew.&nbsp;&nbsp; "I try not to complain. I know this isn't fair,&nbsp; but I also know there is nothing to be done.&nbsp;&nbsp; I try not to be afraid. But I'm just so—so—" But she could not think of a word that was right.&nbsp;&nbsp; She was scared and sad and confused&nbsp; and frustrated and lonely and hopeful. Maybe there was no word that fit.&nbsp; Maybe, for the first time in her life,&nbsp;&nbsp; there was no single word she could hold in her&nbsp; heart to help her understand. And that thought&nbsp;&nbsp; was so sad to Tama, so upsetting, that she&nbsp; felt the hot prickle of tears behind her eyes. "Hey," George said. And for once,&nbsp; his voice was small and soft.&nbsp;&nbsp; "There's a word for that, you know." "There is?" Hope bubbled big&nbsp; and bold in Tama's heart. "Human," George said. He smiled his big smile, and Tama&nbsp; smiled back. She wiped her eyes. She held the word close to her heart and felt&nbsp; less alone than she had only a moment before. "You can't possibly be reading all&nbsp; those books you check out," she said. "No," he replied. "Do you see how long&nbsp; they are? I'm only human, you know." "So then, why come every day?" She laughed. But George was not laughing. And then for the first time she saw him,&nbsp;&nbsp; really saw George, and saw what&nbsp; he held close to his heart. It was her. Tama. They were married, Tama and George, and&nbsp; their first son was born in Minidoka.&nbsp;&nbsp; They hoped for the best. Their love for the family they made was constant,&nbsp;&nbsp; even if the injustice their family&nbsp; was created within was constant, too. To fall in love is already a gift. But&nbsp; to fall in love in a place like Minidoka,&nbsp;&nbsp; a place built to make people feel like&nbsp; they weren't human—that was miraculous. That was humans doing what humans do best. "The miracle is in us," Tama wrote in her journal. "As long as we believe in&nbsp; change, in beauty, in hope." That miracle is hard to find&nbsp; sometimes. But it is in all of us.
