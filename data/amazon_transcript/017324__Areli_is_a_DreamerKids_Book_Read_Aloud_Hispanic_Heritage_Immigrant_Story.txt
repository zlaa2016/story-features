Areli is a Dreamer, a true story by Areli Morales, a DACA recipient; illustrations by Luisa Uribe At Abuela's house, Saturdays were&nbsp; filled with family and sunshine.&nbsp;&nbsp; Areli was good at playing al escondite,&nbsp; and even better at chasing chickens.&nbsp;&nbsp; She could outrun her big brother, Alex, and&nbsp; their primas. When evening came, she sat&nbsp;&nbsp; around a noisy table and ate mounds of tortillas&nbsp; and pollo con frijoles for supper—her favorite! But Sundays were different. On Sundays,&nbsp;&nbsp; Areli and Alex sat in the stuffy living room,&nbsp; waiting and waiting for the phone to ring. Briiiing! Briiing! "It's your mamá and papá,"&nbsp; said Abuela. "From America!" "Hola, Mamá. Hola, Papá,"&nbsp; said Areli in a small voice. "Hola, mi niña," said Mamá. "Did you get the toys we sent you?" asked&nbsp; Papá. Papá always asked about the toys. "Si," said Areli. "Gracias, Mamá. Gracias,&nbsp; Papá." Mamá and Papá had been away so long,&nbsp;&nbsp; they felt like strangers. "When are you&nbsp; coming home?" she asked. She knew the answer. "Oh, mi'ja, we can't come back to Mexico,"&nbsp;&nbsp; said Mamá. "We're trying to get&nbsp; you here to America. With us." "Your mamá and I dream of the&nbsp; day we will all be together,"&nbsp;&nbsp; said Papá. "We will send for you soon. I promise." When Areli hung up the phone,&nbsp; Alex tried to make her smile. "Mamá and Papá think about&nbsp; us all the time," he said. "They're working hard, mi'ja," Abuela said, "for&nbsp; all of us. They want us to have a better life." "But why will Alex go to&nbsp; America first, and then me?"&nbsp;&nbsp; Areli asked. Abuela had told&nbsp; her the answer many times. "Alex was born in America, so he can come and&nbsp; go. You were born here, so it's harder for you." "How hard?" Abuela didn't answer. Instead,&nbsp; she wrapped Areli in her arms. "Your mamá and papá want you all&nbsp; to be together. As a family." But they weren't all together&nbsp; as a family that year.&nbsp;&nbsp; Mamá and Papá didn't come back for Areli's&nbsp; birthday. Or Día de los Muertos. Or even Navidad. Then one night, Alex took out his suitcase. "Where are you going?" Areli&nbsp; asked. But she already knew. "You know where I'm going," Alex&nbsp; said. "I'm going to Nueva York!" He flashed her a big grin and gave her a drawing&nbsp; of the two of them in the yard with the chickens. "So you won't forget me," he said. A few days later, he was gone.&nbsp;&nbsp; Now, when she waited for calls from&nbsp; Mamá and Papá, Areli had to wait alone. That fall, Areli started kindergarten at the&nbsp; school near her abuela's house. She was so&nbsp;&nbsp; proud to be at school! She learned to tie her&nbsp; shoes and write the letters of the alphabet.&nbsp;&nbsp; She played al escondite with her new friends.&nbsp; She barely thought of Nueva York at all... ...until the day Abuela greeted&nbsp; her with tears in her eyes. "Areli, mi'ja. You're leaving us," she said.&nbsp; "Your mamá and papá are sending someone to&nbsp;&nbsp; take you to New York. To live with them&nbsp; at last." Abuela's voice was shaking. "I don't want to go to New York!"&nbsp; Areli said. "I want to stay here with&nbsp;&nbsp; you! And my friends. And my home.&nbsp; Please don't make me go, Abuela." "If I could take care of you here,&nbsp; I would, my sweet. But I am old,&nbsp;&nbsp; and there is no future for you in Mexico. You&nbsp; don't understand now, but someday you will." The next Saturday night's dinner was very&nbsp; sad. Areli said goodbye to her cousins. She said goodbye to her friends. She said goodbye to the&nbsp; mountains and the chickens. "I'll never forget you," she said. On Sunday morning, Abuela woke Areli at&nbsp; dawn. They held each other very tightly. A man with kind eyes was waiting&nbsp; for Areli in the doorway. "This man is a friend of your mamá and papa's,&nbsp; and he will take you to America," Abuela said. "Come, Areli," he said. Areli stayed where she was.&nbsp; The man was a stranger to her. "Be brave, Areli," Abuela said. Areli&nbsp; gave Abuela one more fierce hug. "I love you, mi'ja." "I love you, Abuela!" said Areli. Then she left for her new country. Mamá cried very hard when she saw Areli. Areli ran to her, but Mamá didn't look&nbsp; like her picture at Abuela's house. Her hair was a different color, and it was curly. Papá had lines around his eyes. They held each other for a long&nbsp; time. Areli did not want to let go. "Areli!" Alex said. "You made it! I can't wait&nbsp; to show you our house!" Alex was a lot taller,&nbsp;&nbsp; but he still had a smile that spread ear to ear. Everything in New York was bigger and&nbsp; faster and noisier than in the mountains.&nbsp;&nbsp; It wasn't home at all. Areli had to go to school, but the&nbsp; teachers did not know her name.&nbsp;&nbsp; She couldn't speak English, and when&nbsp; she tried, the words came out all wrong.&nbsp;&nbsp; She made friends with the other&nbsp; girls who could speak Spanish. But she couldn't stop kids from teasing her. "Areli's an illegal. They&nbsp; should take her back to Mexico." "Areli can't read!" "She's so backward!" "Her mom cleans my house. She's our maid!" Areli was ashamed. She had&nbsp; never felt this way before. "No te preocupes, Areli," her friends&nbsp; said. "No pueden hacerte daño." When she went home that night,&nbsp;&nbsp; Areli asked her mother what the kids&nbsp; meant when they said she was illegal. "Illegal means against the law," her mother began. "I'm not against the law!" Areli said. "Of course you're not," said her mother. "But&nbsp; you were born in Mexico. So even though you&nbsp;&nbsp; are growing up here in America, you&nbsp; are not a citizen of this country." "Do I get to be a citizen someday?" asked Areli. "I hope so, mi'ja. I hope so." Her mother wouldn't say anything else. So Areli&nbsp; asked Alex why she needed to be a citizen. "If you're a citizen, you can do&nbsp; whatever Americans do," he said. "What if I'm not?" Areli asked. She&nbsp; wasn't sure she wanted to hear the answer. "Then I guess you could be taken back to Mexico,"&nbsp; said Alex. He wouldn't look at her. "Without us." Areli didn't understand. But she knew she should&nbsp; stay quiet about her first home in Mexico.&nbsp;&nbsp; She did not want to be sent&nbsp; back to Mexico all by herself.&nbsp;&nbsp; She did not want to break the&nbsp; law just by being who she was. Areli had to work extra hard to learn her&nbsp; lessons in a new language. It wasn't easy. By second grade, she could count by fives. In third grade, she learned&nbsp; about the Constitution. By fourth grade, she could write&nbsp; a book report and read it aloud. Pretty soon, it was hard for Areli to remember how&nbsp; the sun shone on the mountains at Abuela's house.&nbsp;&nbsp; Now she spoke English like&nbsp; any other girl from America.&nbsp;&nbsp; She played jump-rope games and watched TV shows. She and Alex could get anywhere&nbsp; in the city on the subway.&nbsp;&nbsp; They rode the F train all the way to Coney Island,&nbsp; where she saw the ocean for the first time. Every Fourth of July, she was mesmerized by&nbsp; the fireworks in the sky. She was a New Yorker. In fifth grade, Areli's&nbsp; class went on a field trip. They got on a boat and&nbsp; sailed into the Hudson River.&nbsp;&nbsp; Areli's heart beat fast to see Ellis Island,&nbsp; where so many immigrants had come before her. On the tour of Ellis Island, Areli learned a lot. "More than twelve million immigrants passed&nbsp; through here on their way to America," said&nbsp;&nbsp; the tour guide. "Almost every one of us has family&nbsp; that came to this country from a foreign land." On the boat ride back home, Areli thought about&nbsp; the tour guide's words. She pictured the millions&nbsp;&nbsp; of other people who had made the long journey&nbsp; to America, and how Lady Liberty welcomed them&nbsp;&nbsp; with her shining torch. She did not feel illegal.&nbsp; She felt like she was part of something very big. On Saturday, she called her abuela in Mexico. "Are you working hard?" Abuela asked. "Very hard," Areli said. She told&nbsp; Abuela all about Ellis Island.&nbsp;&nbsp; "Those immigrants are part of America. Remember&nbsp; when you said I might have a brighter future here,&nbsp;&nbsp; Abuela? I think I understand&nbsp; now. And I think you were right." That night, Areli looked out over the&nbsp; lights of New York City and dreamed&nbsp;&nbsp; of what she might do someday. She&nbsp; might be a writer and tell her story.&nbsp;&nbsp; She might be a teacher and help children&nbsp; who found themselves in this new land.
