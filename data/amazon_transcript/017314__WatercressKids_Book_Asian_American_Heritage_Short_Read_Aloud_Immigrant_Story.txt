Watercress by Andrea Wang, pictures by Jason Chin We are in the old Pontiac, the red paint&nbsp; faded by years of glinting Ohio sun,&nbsp;&nbsp; pelting rain, and biting snow. The tops of the cornstalks make&nbsp; lines that zigzag across the horizon. Mom shouts, "Look!" and the car comes to an abrupt, jerking stop. Mom's eyes are as sharp as&nbsp; the tip of a dragon's claw. Dad's eyes grow wide. "Watercress!" they exclaim,&nbsp; two voices heavy with memories. From the depths of the trunk, they&nbsp; unearth a brown paper bag, rusty scissors, and a longing for China. They haul us out of the back seat. We are told to untie our sneakers, peel&nbsp; off our socks, and roll up our jeans. We have to help them gather it. The water in the ditch is cold. It stings my ankles and the mud&nbsp; squelches up between my toes. A car passes by and I duck my&nbsp; head hoping it's no one I know. My parents cut bunches of the small plant,&nbsp; long stringy stems with leaves round as coins. My big brother yanks watercress up by the handful,&nbsp;&nbsp; roots dripping dirty water onto my&nbsp; shirt, and thrusts it close to my face. There are tiny snails clinging to the underside. I squirm away. The bag in my hands grows heavier and heavier&nbsp; with the weight of all the watercress. The paper is soaked and I'm half afraid&nbsp; half hopeful that the bottom will split,&nbsp;&nbsp; sending all the plants back down into the muck. Finally, we load everything,&nbsp; the soggy bag, my sopping shirt,&nbsp;&nbsp; our sodden selves, into the car and head home. Our original destination is long forgotten,&nbsp; a memory of something unfinished. On the dinner table that&nbsp; night is a dish of watercress,&nbsp;&nbsp; glistening with garlicky oil&nbsp; and freckled with sesame seeds. The mud and the snails are long gone but&nbsp; I still don't want to eat it. Any of it. I only want to eat vegetables&nbsp; from the grocery store. Mom and Dad press me to try some. "It is fresh," Dad says. "It is free," Mom says. I shake my head. Free is bad. Free is hand-me-down clothes and roadside trash-heap furniture and now, dinner from a ditch. Mom sighs and disappears into her&nbsp; room, returning with an old photo. "My family," she says, "from before." We stare. Mom never talks about her China family. She points to a small boy as&nbsp; thin as a stem of watercress. "My little brother. Your uncle." We hold our breaths. Mom never&nbsp; told us what happened to him. "During the great famine," she says,&nbsp; "we ate anything we could find, but it was still not enough." I look from my uncle's hollow face&nbsp; to the watercress on the table&nbsp;&nbsp; and I am ashamed of being ashamed of my family. I take a bite of the watercress and it&nbsp; bites me back with its spicy, peppery taste. It is delicate and slightly bitter, like Mom's memories of home.
