Today we'll be reading Stone Soup by Jon J. Muth. Stone Soup Retold and illustrated by Jon J. Muth Three monks, Hok, Lok, and Siew,&nbsp; traveled along a mountain road.&nbsp;&nbsp; They talked about cat whiskers,&nbsp; the color of the sun, and giving. "What makes one happy, Siew?"&nbsp; asked Hok, the youngest monk. Old Siew, who was the wisest,&nbsp; said, "Let's find out." The sound of a bell brought their gaze&nbsp; to the rooftops of a village below.&nbsp;&nbsp; They could not see from so high above that&nbsp; the village had been through many hard times.&nbsp;&nbsp; Famine, floods, and war had made the&nbsp; villagers weary and untrusting of strangers.&nbsp;&nbsp; They had even become&nbsp; suspicious of their neighbors. The villagers worked hard,&nbsp; but only for themselves. There was a farmer. A tea merchant. A scholar. A seamstress. A doctor. A carpenter... and many others. But they had little to do with one another. When the monks reached the foot of the mountain,&nbsp; the villagers disappeared into their houses.&nbsp;&nbsp; No one came to the gates to greet them. And when the people saw them enter the&nbsp; village, they closed their windows tight. The monks knocked on the door of the first house.&nbsp; There was no answer. Then the house went dark. They knocked on a second door&nbsp; and the same thing happened. It happened again and again,&nbsp; from one house to the next. "These people do not know&nbsp; happiness," they all agreed. "But today," said Siew, his face bright as the&nbsp; moon, "we will show them how to make stone soup." They gathered twigs and branches and made a fire.&nbsp;&nbsp; They placed a small tin pot on top and&nbsp; filled it with water from the village well. A brave little girl who had&nbsp; been watching came to them. "What are you doing?" she asked. "We are gathering twigs," said Lok. "We are making a fire," said Hok. "We are making stone soup and we need&nbsp; three round, smooth stones," said Siew. The little girl helped the monks look around the&nbsp; courtyard until they found just the right ones.&nbsp;&nbsp; Then they put them in the water to cook. "These stones will make&nbsp; excellent soup," said Siew.&nbsp;&nbsp; "But this very small pot&nbsp; won't make much I'm afraid." "My mother has a bigger pot," said the girl. The little girl ran home. As she started to&nbsp; take a pot, her mother asked what she was doing. "The three strangers are making soup from&nbsp; stones," she said. "They need our biggest pot." "Hmm," said the girl's mother. "Stones are easy&nbsp; to come by. I'd like to learn how to do that!" The monks poked the coals. As smoke drifted up,&nbsp; the neighbors peered out from their windows.&nbsp;&nbsp; The fire and the large pot in the middle&nbsp; of the village was a true curiosity! One by one, the people of the village came&nbsp; out to see just what this stone soup was. "Of course, old-style stone soup should be&nbsp; well seasoned with salt and pepper," said Hok. "That is true," said Lok as he stirred the&nbsp; giant pot filled with water and stones.&nbsp;&nbsp; "But we have none..." "I have some salt and pepper!" said the&nbsp; scholar, his eyes big with curiosity.&nbsp;&nbsp; He disappeared and came back with salt&nbsp; and pepper and even a few other spices. Siew took a taste. "The last time we&nbsp; had soup stones of this size and color,&nbsp;&nbsp; carrots made the broth very sweet." "Carrots?" said a woman from the back. "I&nbsp; may have a few carrots! But just a few."&nbsp;&nbsp; And off she ran. She returned with as many carrots&nbsp; as she could carry and dropped them into the pot. "Do you think it would be&nbsp; better with onions?" asked Hok. "Oh, yes, maybe an onion would taste&nbsp; good," said a farmer, and he hurried off.&nbsp;&nbsp; He returned in a moment with five big onions,&nbsp; and he dropped them into the bubbling soup. "Now, that's a fine soup!" he said. The villagers all nodded their heads,&nbsp; as the smell was very agreeable. "But if we only had some mushrooms," said Siew,&nbsp; rubbing his chin. Several villagers licked their&nbsp;&nbsp; lips. A few dashed away and returned with fresh&nbsp; mushrooms, noodles, pea pods, and cabbages. Something magical began to&nbsp; happen among the villagers.&nbsp;&nbsp; As each person opened their heart to&nbsp; give, the next person gave even more.&nbsp;&nbsp; And as this happened, the soup grew&nbsp; richer and smelled more delicious. "I imagine the Emperor would suggest&nbsp; we add dumplings!" said one villager. "And bean curd!" said another. "What about cloud ear and mung&nbsp; beans and yams?" cried some others. "And taro root and winter melon and&nbsp; baby corn!" cried other villagers. "Garlic!" "Ginger root!" "Soy sauce!" "Lily buds!" "I have some! I have some!" people cried out. And&nbsp; off they ran, returning with all they could carry. The monks stirred and the pot bubbled.&nbsp;&nbsp; How good it smelled! How good it would&nbsp; taste! How giving the villagers had become! At last, the soup was ready. The villagers&nbsp; gathered together. They brought rice and steamed&nbsp;&nbsp; buns. They brought lychee nuts and sweet cakes.&nbsp; They brought tea to drink, and they lit lanterns. Everyone sat down to eat. They had not been&nbsp;&nbsp; together for a feast like this for&nbsp; as long as anyone could remember. After the banquet, they told stories, sang&nbsp; songs, and celebrated long into the night. Then they unlocked their doors and took the monks&nbsp;&nbsp; into their homes and gave them&nbsp; very comfortable places to sleep. In the gentle spring morning, everyone gathered&nbsp; together near the willows to say farewell. "Thank you for having us as your guests,"&nbsp; said the monks. "You have been most generous." "Thank you," said the villagers. "With the gifts&nbsp; you have given, we will always have plenty.&nbsp;&nbsp; You have shown us that&nbsp; sharing makes us all richer."
