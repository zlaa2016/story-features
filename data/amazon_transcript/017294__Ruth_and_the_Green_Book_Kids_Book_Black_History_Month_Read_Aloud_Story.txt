"Ruth and the Green Book" by Calvin&nbsp; Alexander Ramsey with Gwen Strauss,&nbsp;&nbsp; illustrations by Floyd Cooper. It was a BIG day at our house when Daddy&nbsp; drove up in our very own automobile— a 1952 Buick! It is the most beautiful&nbsp; color. Daddy calls it Sea Mist Green. He bought it for his new job, but first, we planned to go on a trip&nbsp; to visit Grandma in Alabama. I was so excited to travel across&nbsp; the country! I packed my own bag,&nbsp;&nbsp; and Mama said I could take Brown Bear with me for&nbsp; company, even though I'm almost too old for him. As we drove out of Chicago, it felt funny to see the neighborhood disappear and then the streets and then the buildings. I love all the green grass and hills and trees.&nbsp;&nbsp; Daddy told lots of stories about when he&nbsp; was a boy in Alabama. He made us laugh. Eventually, we stopped and had a&nbsp; picnic lunch that Mama had packed. She had been cooking all week&nbsp; to get ready for the trip. After driving for a long time,&nbsp; we stopped at a service station&nbsp;&nbsp; to fill up on gas. As Daddy was&nbsp; paying the station attendant,&nbsp;&nbsp; Mama asked for the key to the restroom.&nbsp; The man said we couldn't use the restrooms&nbsp;&nbsp; because they were for whites only. Mama and I&nbsp; had to go into the woods. I was embarrassed,&nbsp;&nbsp; but Mama said the people who should be ashamed&nbsp; of themselves were those service station owners. I was tired when Daddy stopped the automobile.&nbsp; I asked Mama where we were, and she said we were&nbsp;&nbsp; going to spend the night in a real hotel.&nbsp; We watched Daddy talking to the man behind&nbsp;&nbsp; the desk. Daddy was smiling and pointing to us.&nbsp; But the man shook his head and turned his back. Daddy came back out and slammed the car&nbsp; door. He never slammed the door before,&nbsp;&nbsp; so I knew he was mad. I wondered what&nbsp; happened, but I knew somehow it wasn't&nbsp;&nbsp; a good time to ask him. Mama gave me a&nbsp; look that said, "Ruth, just keep quiet." So that's what I did. We kept on driving through the night.&nbsp; Mama took a turn so Daddy could sleep. I fell asleep with Brown Bear as my pillow. We must have pulled off the&nbsp; road in the middle of the night, because when I woke in the morning, we were all&nbsp; curled up in the car. I was stiff and hungry.&nbsp;&nbsp; Mama gave us cold biscuits and jam for breakfast.&nbsp; She said we should sing to cheer ourselves up. We sang a lot that day as we crossed the country. We had picnics at roadside stops for lunch and dinner because all the restaurants had signs in the windows that said we couldn't eat there. It seemed like there were "White Only" signs&nbsp; everywhere outside of our Chicago neighborhood. I felt homesick, and I hugged&nbsp; Brown Bear close all day. When we crossed into Tennessee, Daddy&nbsp; said his friend, Eddy, lived close by. He was sure we could sleep there. Eddy was so happy to see us! He gave me a big hug and told me that he knew me when I was real little. His wife, Alice, cooked us a warm meal, and I ate&nbsp; too much. Daddy and Eddy used to play music in a&nbsp;&nbsp; band before they went off and fought in the war.&nbsp; They talked about how they traveled a lot back&nbsp;&nbsp; then and how they always had a hard time finding&nbsp; places to rest and eat. Daddy shook his head and&nbsp;&nbsp; said he had hoped that the war had changed&nbsp; things, but now he could see he was wrong. That night I went to sleep to the sound&nbsp; of Daddy and Eddy playing music together. Brown Bear and I were happy to sleep&nbsp; in a real bed with a real pillow. The next day as we loaded the car, I heard&nbsp; Eddy talking quietly to Mama and Daddy. Eddy warned them about what was&nbsp; ahead, someone called Jim Crow. I heard Eddy tell them that however bad it was&nbsp; so far, it was only going to get worse for us,&nbsp;&nbsp; maybe even dangerous, as we went farther&nbsp; south. Then Eddy saw me listening,&nbsp;&nbsp; and he picked me up and told me not&nbsp; to worry. He said that I should look&nbsp;&nbsp; out for Esso service stations along the road,&nbsp; because the people there would be nice to us. In the car, I asked Daddy who Jim Crow was,&nbsp; and he explained that "Jim Crow" wasn't a who. It was a bunch of ugly laws forbidding&nbsp; blacks and whites from mixing in any way. I asked, "Why don't they want our business? Wasn't our money just the same?" It hurt my feelings to be so unwelcome. But Mama sighed and reminded me that my job&nbsp; that day was to look for an Esso station. I spotted an Esso station near the Georgia&nbsp; border and sang out for Daddy to stop. Daddy filled the car up and asked the man if&nbsp; he knew where we could sleep for the night. The man showed us a pamphlet called&nbsp; The Negro Motorist Green Book. He explained this book was started by a postman,&nbsp; Mr. Victor H. Green, to help black people who&nbsp;&nbsp; were traveling. It lists places in lots of&nbsp; states where we would be welcome to sleep,&nbsp;&nbsp; eat, shop, get a haircut—and all&nbsp; kinds of other information besides. Right away, Daddy bought our very&nbsp; own copy for seventy-five cents. Mama read about a place&nbsp; nearby called a "tourist home" where we could stay for&nbsp; the last night of our trip. Daddy called ahead and sure enough,&nbsp; the lady told us to come right away. Then Mama gave me the Green Book and&nbsp; said I was in charge of keeping it. I couldn't stop reading it— all those places in all those states where we could go and not&nbsp; worry about being turned away. We reached the tourist home in&nbsp; the early evening. Mrs. Melody,&nbsp;&nbsp; the owner, gave us a big smile when she&nbsp; opened the door. It was like coming home. And she wouldn't even let Daddy pay her.&nbsp; She said she welcomed Negro travelers&nbsp;&nbsp; because it was right to help each other&nbsp; out. I'm going to do the same one day! The next morning I hated to&nbsp; say good-bye to Mrs. Melody. But Daddy said we could be at Grandma's house that&nbsp; evening. That made me happy to get going again! It wasn't so easy, though. Our&nbsp; automobile broke down about midday,&nbsp;&nbsp; in the middle of nowhere. People drove right&nbsp; past. I felt empty in the pit of my stomach.&nbsp;&nbsp; I hugged Brown Bear and wished we were already&nbsp; at Grandma's house. But Mama said not to worry,&nbsp;&nbsp; we could figure this out. She unfolded our&nbsp; map and told me the names of nearby towns. She said, "Ruth, look in the Green Book and see&nbsp;&nbsp; if you can find a place in one of&nbsp; those towns that will fix our car." And I did! Daddy gave me a big hug,&nbsp;&nbsp; and then he walked into town to&nbsp; find the garage that would help us. By the time we got the car fixed, it was&nbsp; too late to reach Grandma's house that day. So I looked in the Green Book&nbsp; and picked out an inn nearby that said they welcomed Negro travelers. It was a big house with lots of rooms. Most of the people there were&nbsp; businessmen traveling for their jobs. They each carried a Green Book. They said they could never&nbsp; do their jobs without it! We met a salesman named Jason who has traveled&nbsp; to all the states of the Union for his job. He told such funny stories. Mama laughed so&nbsp; hard, she had tears rolling down her face. For the the first time since we&nbsp; left home, we were really happy. At the inn, I met a little boy&nbsp; who was traveling with his mother. It was the first time he had ever left home, and I could just tell he was scared. I thought of Mrs. Melody, Victor Green, Eddy,&nbsp; and all the other people who had helped us,&nbsp;&nbsp; and then I decided to give Brown Bear to the boy. I told him that Brown Bear was a good traveler.&nbsp; I'm too old for Brown Bear now, and the little boy&nbsp;&nbsp; needed him more than I do. Then I told his mother&nbsp; about the Green Book and showed her our copy. That night, before I went to sleep,&nbsp;&nbsp; I thought about our trip. It was not what&nbsp; I had expected—traveling could be scary,&nbsp;&nbsp; that's for sure! It made me sad that&nbsp; some people were mean to Negroes. But it helped to know that good black people all over the country had&nbsp; pitched in to help each other. It felt like I was part of one big family! Plus, tomorrow I would finally see Grandma and&nbsp; tell her what a great Green Book guide I had been. Thank you so much for joining&nbsp; me for Read Aloud with Mr. Paul.
