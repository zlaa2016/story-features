Tea with Milk by Allen Say From the window in her room, the girl&nbsp; could see the city of San Francisco.&nbsp;&nbsp; She imagined that it was a city of many palaces.&nbsp; And one day her father would take her there,&nbsp;&nbsp; he had promised, riding on a paddle&nbsp; steamer across the shining bay. When she graduated from high school, she wanted&nbsp; to go to college and then live in San Francisco.&nbsp;&nbsp; But her parents were homesick and decided to&nbsp; return to Japan, which was their homeland.&nbsp;&nbsp; The daughter was sad. She could not make&nbsp; friends with any of the other students;&nbsp;&nbsp; they called her gaijin and laughed&nbsp; at her. Gaijin means "foreigner." The woman who taught English conversation&nbsp; did not seem much older than Masako.&nbsp;&nbsp; Maybe she'll be my friend, Masako thought. But&nbsp; the teacher refused to speak English with her.&nbsp;&nbsp; She could not teach an American, she said. So Masako wandered around the empty schoolyard.&nbsp;&nbsp; Small singsong voices came drifting from the&nbsp; classroom, chanting kindergarten English.&nbsp;&nbsp; She wanted to shout at them, "I know the words&nbsp; you are learning! On the following weekend, the matchmaker&nbsp; introduced Masako and her mother to a young&nbsp;&nbsp; banker and his mother. In a fancy restaurant&nbsp; they drank tea and ate lunch and drank more tea.&nbsp;&nbsp; Then the young couple was left alone for&nbsp; the afternoon. The mothers prayed for the&nbsp;&nbsp; marriage of two good families. The matchmaker&nbsp; dreamed of the full fee she could collect. In the evening Masako came home fuming. "Isn't he a charming young man?" her mother asked. "Charming like a catfish!" Masako answered. "His family owns the bank where&nbsp; he works," her mother said. "I won't marry a moneylender!" Masako replied. Masako could not sleep that night. Mother&nbsp; is determined to find a husband for me,&nbsp;&nbsp; she told herself. I could never marry&nbsp; someone like that. Never! What can I do? First thing the next morning, Masako put on the&nbsp; brightest dress she had brought from California&nbsp;&nbsp; and left the house. As she hurried to the&nbsp; bus stop, the villagers stopped and stared. "She looks like a gaijin!" they said loudly. I'm a foreigner in my parents' country,&nbsp;&nbsp; she thought.
