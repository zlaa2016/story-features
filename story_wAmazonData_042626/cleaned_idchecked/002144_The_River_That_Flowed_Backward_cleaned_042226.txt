Beneath them, a young otter named Ali floated lazily on his back, his paws behind his head, watching clouds drift by. Ali loved the water, but he didn't love hard work. Upstream, older otter darted in dove like arrows. They caught fish in one smooth swoop, leaping so high that the sunlight flashed off their wet fur. "Wow," Ali sighed, watching them. "One day I'll swim like that." "But not today. Today's for relaxing." He closed his eyes and let the river carry him. The current was gentle at first, but soon it began to tug faster. When Olly opened his eyes again, the world looked different. The tall pine trees were gone, replaced by marshy bushes and muddy banks. "Oh no," he gasped. "The current carried me downstream." With a huff, he paddled back toward home, but swimming against the current was tough. The water pressed against him, stronger than he remembered. By the time he reached his favorite rock, he was panting. This river is unfair, Olly muttered. Just then, a gentle voice spoke. Uh, that's the way of rivers, young otter. It was Tao, the old turtle, who always seemed to know everything. His shell was covered in moss, and his eyes were kind but sharp. What do you mean? Olly asked. Tao floated beside him. A river never stops moving. If you don't keep swimming forward, it carries you back. Learning works the same way. Stop trying and you lose what you've learned. Olly frowned. So if I stop swimming or stop learning, I go backward. Tao nodded. Exactly. You can float and dream all you want, but if you want to reach the waterfall at the top, you must paddle every day slowly but steadily. Ali thought about that as Tao drifted away. He didn't want to be the otter who always ended up downstream. He wanted to be strong, fast, and fearless. The next morning, Olly got up early. The forest was still quiet, except for the splash of trout and the chatter of kingishers. He took a deep breath and began to swim. At first, it was easy. Then, the current grew stronger and his arms burned. He wanted to stop, but Tao's words echoed in his head. If you don't advance, you will be driven back. So Ali kept going. One stroke, then another. Every day he practiced. Some days the current was too strong. Some days he felt slow and clumsy. Sometimes he even thought of giving up. But whenever he looked downstream, he remembered how far he'd come. He learned new tricks. how to dive deep and rise with speed, how to twist through reads, and how to catch a fish mid jump. Slowly, he began to feel the water becoming his friend instead of his enemy. One day, as Olly practiced near the rapids, a small otter pup named Pip called out, "Olly, the currents too strong. I can't swim forward," Ali dove toward him. "Don't stop paddling," he shouted. Keep your body straight and push against the current. You can do it, Pip tried, trembling with effort. Slowly, with Olly swimming beside him, he made it to the calmer water. You did it, Ali cheered. Olly grinned. I used to float all day and let the river push me wherever it wanted. Then Tao taught me that learning is like swimming upstream. If you stop, you get pulled back. So now I just keep going no matter how hard it gets. Pip smiled shily. Will you teach me? Of course, said Olly. We'll both keep swimming forward together. From that day on, Olly and Pip practiced every morning. Sometimes the current was strong, sometimes it was calm, but they never stopped moving forward. Tao often watched them from a nearby rock, smiling quietly to himself. As the sun dipped behind the trees, Ali felt proud. Not just because he was strong, but because he understood something important. The river never stops flowing, and neither should we.
